Core Message
Spine
Most senior women know, cerebrally, that careers have timelines. What they don't know is that the institution already has a clearer view of that timeline than they do — and that the infrastructure they've been standing on (the title, the EA, the brand, the social capital, the relationships that answered their calls) is not theirs. It belongs to the institution. This episode is about what it costs to find that out too late — and what you can start building now, while you still have access to all of it.
The Distinction
This is not a "plan your retirement" conversation. It is not an "you can have it all" conversation either — Elsie calls that a lie explicitly, and it lands. The distinction is between financial security (your salary continues) and financial flexibility (you could make a free choice tomorrow). Most senior women have the first. Almost none have built the second. That is the gap this episode names.
Operating Principle
Start building what you need to survive outside the institution while you are still inside it — your financial structures, your external visibility, your relationships that are connected to you not your title, your skills that don't require an EA and a budget to deploy. The time to build that infrastructure is when you have the income, the access, and the momentum. Not when you need it.
The Cost
When a senior woman doesn't understand this, she arrives at the moment of transition — chosen or not — and discovers she has been standing on borrowed infrastructure the entire time. The EA disappears. The relationships become transactional. The credibility that walked into rooms before her was the institution's brand, not hers. She feels untethered. Elsie uses that exact word, and it is the most honest word in the episode.

Recording Review
What Landed Better Than the Script
The DFI story. Nothing in the framework anticipated this, and it is the strongest material in the recording. Elsie walked away from an MD role at Citi to take a position at a development finance institution in Africa — left without another job, told Citi no twice when they offered the role back — and three weeks before flying, found out the title structure had been quietly downgraded. She proposed an elegant compromise. It was refused. She walked. That story is the episode's proof of concept: a woman who has tested her own values under real pressure and held the line. Let it run uninterrupted at full length.
Where the Recording Diverged — Worth Keeping
The sponsorship section ran longer and richer than scripted. Elsie's definition — "a sponsor is spending their social capital on you" — and her mechanics for finding one (find what lights you up and let people who are passionate about the same things find you) are better than anything in the original framework because they come from her lived mechanics, not a prompted answer. Keep this section. It is not a detour — it is the institutional knowledge section delivering exactly what was promised. Also: Uche's spontaneous disclosure about the tech company she joined and quickly knew was wrong — this came out naturally and worked exactly as intended, without the scripted MTN version.

Edit Instructions
Overall Instruction
This is one of the strongest guest episodes in the catalogue. The priority is not cutting for time — it is protecting the emotional architecture. Target runtime: 70–80 minutes. The DFI story (Elsie's departure from Citi, the development finance institution that fell through, the conversations with the CEO) must run uninterrupted at full length. Do not compress it. It is the reason people will recommend this episode. Everything else can tighten around it.
Tone to Preserve
This is a conversation between two women who have known each other for decades and are being honest about things they don't usually say publicly. Elsie speaks with warmth, precision, and occasional dry humour — "life is short, why spend it doing stuff that doesn't serve you?" and "we are going to finish this money here" are both spoken lightly, with a smile, not as declarations. The intimacy between the two of them is audible and should not be edited out. Uche's moments of genuine reaction ("I'm in awe of this, by the way") are real and should stay.
Two Separate Recording Files — Join Before Editing
This episode was recorded in two separate files due to a power and connection interruption mid-recording. File 1 ends at the interruption point — approximately two thirds through the raw recording. File 2 picks up with Elsie recapping where she left off: "I wasn't ready. I hadn't built anything that would sustain me beyond the corporate world." Join both files into a single timeline before beginning the edit. The join point is clean — Elsie restarts mid-thought so there will be a small overlap to trim. Do not call attention to the interruption in the final edit. The audience should not hear it.

Show Notes
Opening Line
At 40, most senior women feel like they're building upward. The next role, the next market, the next challenge. What they don't realise is that the institution already has a clearer view of their runway than they do.
Episode Description
Elsie Akinsanya spent over a decade at Citi as a Managing Director in HR — inside the rooms where decisions about people's careers were being made. She knows what organisations say about their senior leaders in rooms those leaders are not in. She also knows what it feels like to leave a role like that, to discover that much of the infrastructure you thought was yours belonged to the institution, and to figure out what you actually have to build for yourself. This is an honest conversation about career timelines, borrowed authority, financial flexibility, and what women at 35, 40, and 50 need to be building while they still have the title, the income, and the access to build it.
Key Themes
How organisations categorise senior talent — and why most leaders don't know which category they're in
The difference between sponsorship and mentorship, and why performance alone stops working above a certain level
What disappears when you leave a senior role that you didn't realise you were standing on
The difference between financial security and financial flexibility — and why most senior women have one and not the other
What to build before you need it, and why 40 is the right moment to start
