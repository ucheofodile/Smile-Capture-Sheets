Core Message
Spine
Lots of people want to become CEOs. Very few tell us what it was actually like the first time they became one. Laureine Guilao worked alongside a CEO before unexpectedly finding herself in the seat. This episode follows what happened next: what proximity genuinely prepared her for, what she could only discover once the responsibility was hers, and what she understands differently now that she's been on both sides of the chair.
The Distinction — What This Episode Says That Hasn't Been Said
Most first-time CEO content is aspirational or retrospectively polished. This episode is neither. It gets inside a specific, unexpected route into the role — what proximity can genuinely prepare you for, what you can only learn by carrying the responsibility yourself, what the job consumes that nobody warns you about, and what happens when the title is gone.

The episode has three unusually strong discoveries. The 10% to 50% stakeholder management revelation is the practical one — one of the episode's most practically useful insights. But what makes that passage genuinely distinctive isn't merely that stakeholder management takes more time than expected. It's the central contradiction of the CEO job: internally, you are the boss; externally and upwards, you still have bosses. The CEO carries final responsibility without necessarily having final freedom. Laureine describes having to execute board decisions back into an organisation whose employees experience those decisions as hers. That is considerably more interesting than the statistic alone.

"I do remember you telling me it would be lonely. And I was like, she has me, I'm her friend" is the human discovery — she found that having people around you, even people who understand the job, doesn't entirely remove the loneliness of carrying the decision yourself. "They're not inviting you. They're inviting the role" is the identity discovery — the standing she realised belonged partly to the position rather than the person.

Together they cover the work she didn't know would consume so much of the job, the loneliness she couldn't understand from outside the seat, and the standing she discovered belonged partly to the position rather than the person.

And crucially: this is not a story about the job humbling her. Laureine was prepared, capable, and enjoyed the role. There were still things she could only know once she became CEO. That's a more interesting first-time CEO story than the conventional one — and the capture sheet should protect that distinction all the way through.
What This Episode Is Not
Not a resilience or comeback story, even though an involuntary exit is present. Not a startup founder episode, even though she built from scratch. Not a general leadership conversation. The episode is specifically about the reality of becoming a CEO for the first time — what you think you know going in, what the job actually asks of you once you're there, and what you understand differently afterward.
Floor Connection
Growth is the primary floor — not because Laureine entered the role unprepared, but because the role required her to stretch capabilities she already had and develop others under the weight of final responsibility: becoming more decisive when collaboration tipped into over-listening, managing the scale and consequence of stakeholder relationships, and learning what could no longer be delegated upwards. Foundation appears in what she says was most vulnerable when work became consuming: exercise, eating well and taking care of herself. Support is explicit in her discovery of CEO loneliness and the network she built around herself — friends in business, fellow executives at the Chamber of Commerce, banking MDs in Liberia, board relationships and people outside the company she could talk to honestly.

The adjacency hypothesis in the guide assumed proximity would produce false confidence that collapsed in the role. Laureine's actual answer inverted this: proximity genuinely prepared her well. What the role corrected was over-listening — being so collaborative that she wasn't decisive enough. That's a better and more surprising insight than the guide anticipated. Do not edit this toward the original hypothesis.

Laureine is not performing a polished CEO persona — she's relaxed, self-aware, occasionally funny. The edit should make the conversation feel tighter, not stiffer.

Episode Description
Laureine Guilao was Deputy CEO at MTN Liberia. She was close to the seat — close enough to think she understood it. Then a career detour placed her in the CEO chair itself, running TipMe Liberia, the first app-based mobile wallet in the country. She built it from scratch during COVID, led it for five years, and eventually chose to leave. This conversation moves through three stages: what proximity teaches you about the CEO job, and what it conceals; what the job actually consumed once she was in it; and what she now understands about leadership that she couldn't have understood before she held the title herself.
Key Themes
What working alongside a CEO teaches you — and what it doesn't show you at all
Why stakeholder management consumed 50% of the job she expected it to take 10%
The difference between being invited as a person and being invited as a title
How collaboration can become indecision at CEO level
What a detour into the role teaches you that a planned path cannot
Leadership Infrastructure — what she had, what she was missing, and what she'd build differently
Guest Bio Line
Laureine Guilao is a senior business leader with experience across telecoms and fintech in West Africa. She served as Deputy CEO of MTN Liberia and as founding CEO of TipMe Liberia, the first app-based mobile wallet in Liberia. She is currently founder of CONA Consulting.

"The Job After the Job" — what happens to identity, access and invitations when the title is gone. Laureine's "inviting the role" insight is the seed of a full solo episode.
"The Job I Thought I Understood" — a recurring format. First-time board chairs, first-time ministers, first-time entrepreneurs. The pattern: what did you think the role was before you had it, and what did you only learn by living it? This conversation is the template.
Financial security vs financial flexibility — Laureine's comment about needing to see a company's P&L before taking another CEO role connects to the Financial Floor series.

Leadership Infrastructure — Notes for Beneath the Smile
Laureine's experience illustrates a specific Infrastructure pattern: strong Support (board relationships, peer network, trusted friendships) paired with a fragile Foundation (physical self-care, exercise, and rest were the first things to go when the job took over). She identified this herself in relation to Beneath the Smile. The Recovery floor is also live — she describes the post-MTN period as "the most traumatic experience of my life" before finding her footing again through reputation and network. The episode could be used in future as a case study for how Infrastructure that looks strong externally can be quietly fragile at the base.
