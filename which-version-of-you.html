Bring Your Authentic Self to Work. Really?

Solo · Uche Ofodile · Recorded: [DATE, confirm] · Floor: Presence (primary), Foundation (secondary)
1. Overview
Core Message
Spine

Everyone tells you to bring your authentic self to work. But in a meeting where a very senior stakeholder professed his love for her in front of her team, Uche smiled, apologised for not seeing his messages and got the meeting back on track. What she actually felt had become almost irrelevant to how she responded. Everybody adjusts. The episode holds three connected ideas, with equal weight:

  1. Sometimes adjusting is the most sensible thing you can do, even when it feels terrible. The stakeholder meeting was a conscious decision, and the right one. It still felt like dying inside.
  2. Sometimes you adapt to other people's expectations so often that choice and habit become hard to tell apart. The low energy story.
  3. When you have power, you shape how other people behave around you without seeing it. The CEO's jokes. Telling people to be authentic does not make the power dynamics disappear.
The Distinction
Most authenticity content is told from below: what the powerful make us do. This episode is told from both sides. Uche has made these calculations herself, and as a CEO she is now the person others adjust for. People laugh harder at her jokes than the jokes deserve, and she may never know what they are really thinking.
Operating Principle
You can make the right decision for the circumstances and still have to live with the discomfort of having made it. And if you are the most senior person in the room, the harder question is how much room you are giving other people to bring themselves.
The Cost
Even a right decision has a cost, and nobody in the room sees it. A leader who has adjusted for so long that it is automatic stops noticing what she gives up. And a leader who assumes "be authentic" is enough never sees how much the people around her are adjusting for her.
This Episode Is Not About
Guardrails for everyone marketing this episode
  • Not a sexual harassment or #MeToo episode. The stakeholder story is the door into the episode, not its subject. No caption, title or comment reply should frame it as harassment, exposé or scandal.
  • Never hint at who the stakeholder was, which country or which sector. Uche says "many years ago" on purpose. Do not answer comments that guess.
  • Not "authenticity is fake" or "stop being yourself." Uche's position is that everybody adjusts and the question is whether you choose to.
  • Not a complaint about other people. The CEO section is the turn: she is on both sides.
  • Not about Uche's current employer or team.
Audience Promise
What a senior woman leaves with
The smile you gave in that meeting was not a failure to be authentic. It was a decision, and it is allowed to have felt terrible. It is worth knowing which of your adjustments are still decisions and which have become habits. And if you lead, the people around you are making the same calculations about you.

5. Show Notes
Not a recap. Each version leads with the idea and names the key messages a listener should leave with. YouTube and Spotify are written differently because people find and read them differently. None of them describes the stakeholder story as harassment.
Key Messages (shared by every version)
What the listener should leave with
  1. Everybody adjusts at work, no matter how senior. Seniority changes the people and the stakes, not the calculation.
  2. Sometimes adjusting is the most sensible thing you can do, and you can make the right call and still have to live with how it felt.
  3. Adapt to other people's expectations often enough and it gets hard to tell choice from habit.
  4. If you lead, people are adjusting for you too. Telling them to be authentic does not make the power dynamics disappear.
  5. What looks like comfort can be discomfort being managed.

We keep telling people to bring their authentic selves to work. But what does that mean when the consequences of being yourself could be serious?

Many years ago, in a very important meeting with my team, a senior stakeholder decided to tell me how he felt about me. I smiled, laughed and got the meeting back on track. This episode is about that smile, and about the adjustments all of us make at work, including the ones people make around me now that I'm the CEO.

In this episode:
• Why everybody adjusts at work, however senior they are
• The extra calculations women make to avoid being called difficult or aggressive
• What happened when I was told I had low energy
• Why people laugh harder at the CEO's jokes
• The difference between choosing to adjust and no longer noticing you do

Au travail, peut-on vraiment être soi-même ?
Description

On nous répète d'être nous-mêmes au travail. Mais qu'est-ce que cela veut dire quand les conséquences peuvent être sérieuses ?

Il y a plusieurs années, lors d'une réunion très importante avec mon équipe, un interlocuteur très haut placé a décidé de me déclarer ses sentiments. J'ai souri, j'ai ri et j'ai ramené la réunion sur les rails. Cet épisode parle de ce sourire, et des ajustements que nous faisons tous au travail, y compris ceux que les gens font autour de moi maintenant que je suis DG.

Dans cet épisode :
• Pourquoi tout le monde s'ajuste au travail, quel que soit son niveau
• Les calculs supplémentaires que font les femmes pour ne pas passer pour « difficiles » ou « agressives »
• Ce qui s'est passé quand on m'a dit que je manquais d'énergie
• Pourquoi on rit plus fort aux blagues de la DG
• La différence entre choisir de s'ajuster et ne plus remarquer qu'on le fait

Tuesday — Launch Post (native upload of Clip 03)
I tell a joke in a meeting and people laugh. Sometimes they laugh hysterically. And I sit there thinking, I know I'm funny, but I'm not that funny. Of course I know what's happening. I'm the CEO. I tell my team all the time that they can challenge me, and I mean it. But I can't announce that everyone should be authentic and assume the power dynamics have disappeared. My title is still my title. I keep wondering what else people are adjusting around me that I will never see.
Saturday / Sunday — Reflective Follow-Up (Clip 01 optional)
Many years ago I was in a very important meeting with a senior stakeholder, my whole team sitting with me, when he decided to tell me how he felt about me. I smiled. I laughed. I apologised for not seeing his messages, and I got the meeting back on track. Inside I was mortified. I was wondering what my team thought, and what would happen to the business if I reacted the way I actually wanted to. Given what was at stake, I still think it was the right decision. It didn't change how I felt.
